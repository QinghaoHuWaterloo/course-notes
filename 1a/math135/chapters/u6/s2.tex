\subsection{Principle of Strong Induction}
Some inductive steps need more than the immediately preceding case. Strong
induction makes all earlier cases available, which is useful for recurrences
that depend on two or more previous terms.

\subsubsection{Why Use a Stronger Inductive Hypothesis?}
\begin{example}
    A sequence $\set{x_n}$ is defined recursively by
    \[
        x_1=4,\qquad x_2=68,\qquad
        x_n=2x_{n-1}+15x_{n-2}\quad\text{for all }n\geq3.
    \]
    Prove the closed form
    \[
        x_n=2\paren{-3}^n+10\cdot5^{n-1}
        \quad\text{for all }n\in\N.
    \]
\end{example}
Let $P(n)$ be the claimed formula. The first case holds because
$2(-3)+10=4=x_1$. However, if we assume only $P(k)$, the recurrence
\[
    x_{k+1}=2x_k+15x_{k-1}\qquad(k\geq2)
\]
still involves $x_{k-1}$, whose closed form is not supplied by that
hypothesis. We need both $P(k)$ and $P(k-1)$, as well as a second base case.
This does not mean ordinary induction is invalid; it means this choice of
inductive hypothesis does not provide everything the step needs.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{The Strong Induction Principle}
\mdfsetup{nobreak=true}
\begin{definition}[Principle of Strong Induction]
    Let $P(n)$ be an open sentence that depends on $n\in\N$. If
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
        \item $P(1)$ is true, and
        \item for every $k\in\N$, if $P(1),P(2),\ldots,P(k)$ are
        all true, then $P(k+1)$ is true,
    \end{enumerate}
    then $P(n)$ is true for all $n\in\N$.
\end{definition}
\mdfsetup{nobreak=false}
The step assumes every case up to $k$, not just the case $k$. It may use
whichever of these earlier cases the argument needs.

To start at an integer $b$ and use several base cases, fix an integer
$B\geq b$. Prove $P(b),P(b+1),\ldots,P(B)$. Then, for arbitrary $k\geq B$,
assume $P(i)$ for every integer $b\leq i\leq k$ and prove $P(k+1)$.
Here $B$ is fixed by the proof; we do not prove base cases for every $B$.

\subsubsection{Returning to the Recurrence}
\begin{remark}[Proof idea]
    Check the two initial terms, then substitute the formulas for both
    $x_k$ and $x_{k-1}$. Group the powers of $-3$ and of $5$ separately.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
    We prove $P(n)$ by strong induction on $n$.

    \textbf{Base cases.} Both $P(1)$ and $P(2)$ hold, since
    \begin{align*}
        2\paren{-3}^1+10\cdot5^0&=-6+10=4=x_1,\\
        2\paren{-3}^2+10\cdot5^1&=18+50=68=x_2.
    \end{align*}

    \textbf{Inductive step.} Let $k\geq2$ be an arbitrary integer.
    Assume $P(i)$ is true for every integer $1\leq i\leq k$, that is,
    \[
        x_i=2\paren{-3}^i+10\cdot5^{i-1}
        \quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq k.
    \]
    In particular, the hypothesis applies to both $k$ and $k-1$.
    Using the recurrence and substituting these two cases,
    \begin{align*}
        x_{k+1}
        &=2x_k+15x_{k-1}\\
        &=2\bracks{2\paren{-3}^k+10\cdot5^{k-1}}
          +15\bracks{2\paren{-3}^{k-1}+10\cdot5^{k-2}}\\
        &=4\paren{-3}^k+30\paren{-3}^{k-1}
          +20\cdot5^{k-1}+150\cdot5^{k-2}\\
        &=\paren{-3}^{k-1}\bracks{4(-3)+30}
          +5^{k-2}\bracks{20\cdot5+150}\\
        &=18\paren{-3}^{k-1}+250\cdot5^{k-2}\\
        &=2\paren{-3}^{k+1}+10\cdot5^k.
    \end{align*}
    This is $P(k+1)$. By strong induction, the formula holds for every
    $n\in\N$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{In practice}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choose the hypothesis from the recurrence.} If the next
    term uses two preceding terms, check both initial cases and make both
    preceding formulas available in the inductive step.
    \item \textbf{Strong induction template.} State $P(n)$ and check the
    required base cases. For arbitrary $k$ beyond them, assume every earlier
    case in the claimed range, prove $P(k+1)$, and conclude by strong induction.
\end{itemize}
