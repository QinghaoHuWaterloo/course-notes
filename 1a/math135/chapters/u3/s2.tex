\subsection{Implication}
An implication specifies what must follow when its hypothesis is true.
Its converse and contrapositive reverse the roles of the two statements
in different ways.
\begin{definition}[Implication]
    The truth value for ``$A$ implies $B$'', written symbolically as $A \implies B$, is defined by the truth table
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
        $A$ & $B$ & $A \implies B$ \\
        \hline
        T & T & T \\
        T & F & F \\
        F & T & T \\
        F & F & T
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    We also refer to $A \implies B$ as an implication.
\end{definition}

In $A \implies B$, the statement $A$ is the \textbf{hypothesis} (what we assume to be true) and
$B$ is the \textbf{conclusion} (what we must then show). The only way for an implication to be false
is a true hypothesis with a false conclusion. When $A$ is false, $A \implies B$ is true no matter
what $B$ is (it is \emph{vacuously} true).

\begin{example}
    \begin{equation*}
        \paren{\forall x \in \R}\bracks{\paren{x > 2 } \implies \paren{x^2 > 4}}
    \end{equation*}
    This is true. If $x > 2$, then $x > 0$, so $x^2 = x \cdot x > 2x > 4$. If $x \leq 2$, the
    hypothesis is false, so the implication is vacuously true for that $x$.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
    An implication with an ``or'' in its hypothesis splits into two implications:
    \begin{equation*}
        \paren{(A \vee B) \implies C}
        \equiv (A \implies C) \wedge (B \implies C).
    \end{equation*}
    This is the logic behind a proof by cases.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Converse and Contrapositive}
\subsubsection{Converse}
The implication $B \implies A$ is called the converse of $A \implies B$.
\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c}
        $A$ & $B$ & $A \implies B$ & $B \implies A$ \\
        \hline
        T & T & T & T \\
        T & F & F & T \\
        F & T & T & F \\
        F & F & T & T
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{remark}
    $(A \implies B) \not\equiv (B \implies A)$: the two columns differ in the second and third rows.
    For instance, $\forall x \in \R, (x > 2) \implies (x^2 > 4)$ is true, but its converse
    $\forall x \in \R, (x^2 > 4) \implies (x > 2)$ is false (take $x = -3$).
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Contrapositive}
The implication $(\neg B) \implies (\neg A)$ is called the contrapositive of $A \implies B$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c|c}
$A$ & $B$ & $\neg A$ & $\neg B$ & $A \implies B$ & $(\neg B) \implies (\neg A)$ \\
\hline
T & T & F & F & T & T \\
T & F & F & T & F & F \\
F & T & T & F & T & T \\
F & F & T & T & T & T
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
    $(A \implies B) \equiv ((\neg B) \implies (\neg A))$: an implication and its contrapositive
    always have the same truth value.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Rewriting and Negating an Implication}
An implication is also logically equivalent to a disjunction, as shown in the following truth table:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
$A$ & $B$ & $\neg A$ & $A \implies B$ & $\neg A \vee B$ \\
\hline
T & T & F & T & T \\
T & F & F & F & F \\
F & T & T & T & T \\
F & F & T & T & T
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
That is, $(A \implies B) \equiv (\neg A \vee B)$.

\begin{remark}
    The negation of an implication is not an implication. Negating $\neg A \vee B$ with De Morgan's Law
    gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \neg (A \implies B) \equiv A \wedge \neg B,
    \end{equation*}
    which matches the only row where $A \implies B$ is false.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{If and Only If}
\begin{definition}[If and Only If]
    The truth value for ``$A$ if and only if $B$'', written symbolically as $A \iff B$, is defined by the truth table

    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
    $A$ & $B$ & $A \iff B$ \\
    \hline
    T & T & T \\
    T & F & F \\
    F & T & F \\
    F & F & T
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

\end{definition}

Thus $A \iff B$ is true exactly when $A$ and $B$ have the same truth value, and
\begin{equation*}
    (A \iff B) \equiv (A \implies B) \wedge (B \implies A).
\end{equation*}
To prove an ``if and only if'' statement, we prove an implication and its converse.
