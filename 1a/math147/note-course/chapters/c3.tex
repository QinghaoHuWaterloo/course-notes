\section{Sequences and Limits}

% ----- Concept map (structure note) -----
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
\textbf{Concept map.}
The $\epsilon$-definition of a limit (\cref{def:limit}) is the hub of the
chapter. From it: a limit is unique (\cref{thm:limit-unique}, via
\cref{prop:eps-forces-equal}); a convergent sequence is bounded
(\cref{thm:convergent-bounded}); and arithmetic passes through limits
(\cref{thm:limit-laws}). Order interacts with limits in
\cref{thm:limit-nonneg,thm:limits-and-order}, which feed the squeeze
theorem (\cref{thm:squeeze}). The chapter's completeness payoff is the
monotone convergence theorem (\cref{thm:monotone-convergence}): it turns a
bounded monotone sequence into a convergent one using the supremum and its
$\epsilon$-characterization (\cref{lem:eps-characterization}) from \S2 ---
the direct bridge back to the real-number axioms. Its first applications
are a recursively defined sequence (\cref{ex:recursive-mct}) and Euler's
number $e$ (\cref{prop:e-limit}). Finally, every subsequence of a
convergent sequence has the same limit (\cref{thm:subsequence-limit}),
which also gives a test for divergence.
\end{quote}
