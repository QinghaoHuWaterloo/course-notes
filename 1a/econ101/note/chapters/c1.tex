\section{The Four Core Principles of Economics}
An \term{economy} allocates scarce resources: what to produce, how to
produce it, who does the work, and who receives the output. The slides use
the stranded group in \emph{Lord of the Flies} to illustrate these questions.
Economics begins with individual decisions and studies how they fit together.
The four principles below provide a way to reason about those decisions.

\subsection{The Cost--Benefit Principle}
\begin{definition}[Economic Surplus]\label[definition]{def:surplus}
The \term{economic surplus} from a decision is its benefit minus its cost,
measured on a common scale and relative to the same alternative.
\[
\text{Economic surplus}=\text{benefit}-\text{cost}.
\]
\end{definition}
The cost--benefit principle says to choose an option over its alternative
when the benefit is at least the cost. Equality means indifference;
strictly greater benefit means a strict gain. With several mutually
exclusive options, a positive surplus is not enough: another option may
have a larger surplus.

\begin{definition}[Willingness to Pay]\label[definition]{def:wtp}
\term{Willingness to pay} is the greatest amount a person would pay to
obtain a benefit, or to avoid a cost. It gives nonmonetary consequences,
such as time saved, a dollar valuation.
\end{definition}
A valuation is not necessarily a payment. An hour saved can be valuable
even when no money changes hands. The slides also note that benefits can
go to someone else and outcomes can be uncertain; assigning values in
those situations requires more care than the simple examples here.
Costs and benefits are \term{economic incentives} that influence choices.
The principle is a guide to reasoning, not a claim that people always
calculate or act this way.

\begin{example}[Gains from Trade]\label[example]{ex:trade}
A buyer values a good at $v$, a seller's relevant cost is $c$, and they
trade at price $p$. Relative to no trade, their surpluses are
\[
\text{Buyer's surplus}=v-p,\qquad
\text{seller's surplus}=p-c.
\]
Both weakly gain when $c\leq p\leq v$; both strictly gain when $c<p<v$.
\end{example}
Adding the surpluses cancels the payment: total surplus is $v-c$.
Price determines how the gain is divided between these two parties.
This explains why an exchange can benefit both, rather than being a
zero-sum transfer. The comparison assumes no additional transaction
costs and does not assess effects on third parties.

\subsection{The Opportunity Cost Principle}
\begin{definition}[Opportunity Cost]\label[definition]{def:opportunity-cost}
The \term{opportunity cost} of a choice is the value of the next best
feasible alternative forgone. Its \term{financial cost} is the money paid
out of pocket; financial cost can miss valuable uses of time or resources.
\end{definition}
Resources are \term{scarce}: using time, money or energy one way prevents
using the same resource another way. Ask ``this option, or what?'' The
relevant alternative is the next best one, not the sum of every possible
use of the resource. In accounting for costs, do not count the same forgone
value twice.

For an hour of gaming, the major cost can be the next best use of the hour,
rather than the hardware or software. An activity with no explicit payment
can therefore have a substantial opportunity cost.

\begin{definition}[Sunk Cost]\label[definition]{def:sunk-cost}
A \term{sunk cost} has already been incurred and cannot be recovered by
changing the current decision. It does not distinguish the current options.
\end{definition}
Ignoring it does not mean the money was unimportant. It means that choosing
one option rather than another cannot change that cost. Subtracting the
same amount from every option leaves their ranking unchanged.

\begin{example}[Attending a Lecture]\label[example]{ex:tuition}
The slides allocate \dollar{4{,}500} tuition across five courses, each with
24 lectures: $4500/(5\cdot24)=\dollar{37.50}$ per lecture.
\end{example}
This is an average allocation, not the additional cost of attending.
Once tuition is nonrefundable, skipping the lecture does not recover
\dollar{37.50}. Compare the benefit of attending with the next best use
of those 80 minutes, such as earning wages.

\subsection{The Production Possibilities Frontier}
A production possibilities frontier shows the trade-offs imposed by
limited resources. It describes what can be achieved, not which
combination a person should prefer.

\begin{definition}[Production Possibilities Frontier]\label[definition]{def:ppf}
A combination of outputs is \term{feasible} if available resources and
technology can produce it. It is \term{efficient} if one output cannot
be increased without reducing another. The \term{production possibilities
frontier} (PPF) consists of the efficient feasible combinations.
\end{definition}
Points inside the frontier leave room to improve an output without
sacrificing the other. Points outside it cannot be achieved with the
current resources and technology.

\begin{example}[Three Hours of Study]\label[example]{ex:ppf}
The slides allow three hours per night for economics and psychology.
Each economics hour adds eight grade points; each psychology hour adds four.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
Economics hours&Psychology hours&Econ improvement&Psych improvement\\
\midrule
3&0&24&0\\
2&1&16&4\\
1&2&8&8\\
0&3&0&12\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}
These are grade improvements, not final grades. With psychology improvement
$P$ horizontally and economics improvement $E$ vertically, the frontier is
$E=24-2P$ for $0\leq P\leq12$.
Moving from $(P,E)=(4,16)$ to $(8,8)$ gains four psychology points and
loses eight economics points. The opportunity cost of one psychology
point is therefore two economics points.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[width=0.75\linewidth,height=6cm,axis lines=left,
 xmin=0,xmax=20,ymin=0,ymax=38,xlabel={Psychology improvement $P$},
 ylabel={Economics improvement $E$},xtick={0,4,8,12,18},ytick={0,8,16,24,36}]
\addplot[thick,domain=0:12] {24-2*x};
\addplot[thick,dashed,domain=0:18] {36-2*x};
\addplot[only marks] coordinates {(4,16) (8,8)};
\node at (axis cs:14,23) {higher productivity};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
The absolute slope measures the trade-off in vertical-axis output per unit
of horizontal-axis output. Here it is constant because the assumed returns
to each study hour are constant. A curved PPF can have a changing trade-off.

The dashed line reproduces the slides' productivity improvement. Increasing
both hourly gains by 50\% gives a frontier from $(0,36)$ to $(18,0)$.
More combinations become feasible, although the opportunity cost remains
two economics points per psychology point. An outward shift does not
necessarily mean the trade-off is unchanged; it happens to be unchanged here.

\subsection{The Marginal Principle}
Binary choices ask ``which option?'' Quantity choices ask ``how much?''
The marginal principle breaks the latter into comparisons of adjacent quantities.

\begin{definition}[Marginal Benefit and Marginal Cost]\label[definition]{def:marginal}
The \term{marginal benefit} of an additional unit is the increase in total
benefit caused by that unit. Its \term{marginal cost} is the increase in
total cost. For the $k$-th unit,
\[
\MB(k)=\TB(k)-\TB(k-1),\qquad
\MC(k)=\TC(k)-\TC(k-1).
\]
\end{definition}
Buying or producing that unit changes surplus by $\MB(k)-\MC(k)$.
This is a comparison of \emph{changes}, not average benefit or average cost.

\paragraph{The rational rule.}
Continue while an additional unit's marginal benefit is at least its
marginal cost. To make this a reliable stopping rule, marginal net benefit
must not rise again after becoming negative. A common sufficient condition
is that $\MB-\MC$ is nonincreasing across the feasible units.
Then surplus rises with positive increments, stays unchanged with zero
increments, and falls with negative increments.

An equality $\MB=\MC$ means the additional unit gives no extra surplus;
including or excluding it can both be optimal. In a discrete problem,
the sign may jump from positive to negative without ever reaching equality.
The slides' ``continue until they are equal'' is therefore best understood
as this marginal comparison, rather than a requirement to find exact equality.

\begin{example}[Nerida's Restaurant]\label[example]{ex:restaurant}
The slides list worker counts from two to seven. A meal sells for
\dollar{25}, food costs \dollar{10} per meal, each worker costs
\dollar{300}, rent costs \dollar{500}, and Nerida's time is valued at
\dollar{1{,}000}, all for the same operating period.
\end{example}
For $M$ meals and $q$ workers, total benefit is $25M$ and total cost is
$10M+300q+1500$. The resulting slide table is:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrrr}
\toprule
Workers&Meals&$\TB$&$\MB$&$\TC$&$\MC$&Surplus\\
\midrule
2&160&4000&---&3700&---&300\\
3&210&5250&1250&4500&800&750\\
4&250&6250&1000&5200&700&1050\\
5&280&7000&750&5800&600&1200\\
6&300&7500&500&6300&500&1200\\
7&310&7750&250&6700&400&1050\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The fourth worker adds $250-210=40$ meals: marginal benefit is
$25\cdot40=1000$, while marginal cost is $300+10\cdot40=700$.
The corresponding marginal surplus is \dollar{300}.

Both $\MB$ and $\MC$ decrease in this example. The marginal surpluses
are \dollar{450}, \dollar{300}, \dollar{150}, zero, and \dollar{-150}. Five and six workers both maximise surplus
at \dollar{1{,}200}; the sixth adds exactly zero. The comparison is limited
to the listed staffing choices, since the slides supply no other output data.
Rent and the value of Nerida's time cancel between these choices because
they are the same in each. This alone does not make them sunk in a separate
decision about opening or closing the restaurant.

\subsection{The Interdependence Principle}
Your best choice depends on its setting. The slides distinguish four
sources of interdependence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Your other choices.} Income, time, energy and attention are
shared. Choosing economics study hours leaves fewer psychology study hours.
\item \textbf{Other people's choices.} People, firms and governments compete
for scarce resources, affecting the opportunities available to one another.
\item \textbf{Other markets.} Conditions in labour, housing and credit markets
can affect choices elsewhere.
\item \textbf{Time.} Past choices and expectations of future opportunities
influence current choices.
\end{enumerate}
The next chapters first hold these other conditions fixed to study a
response to price, then consider what happens when those conditions change.

\subsubsection*{In practice}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Identify the alternative.} Compare costs and benefits on the
same basis; count each forgone value once and exclude unrecoverable past costs.
\item \textbf{Compare increments.} Subtract adjacent totals, then compare
marginal benefit with marginal cost. Check that stopping does not miss later gains.
\item \textbf{Read a PPF.} Check feasibility and efficiency, then calculate
what must be sacrificed for the desired improvement.
\end{itemize}
