\section{Supply and Producer Choice}
Supply describes sellers' quantity decisions at different prices.
The marginal principle still applies, but the additional benefit is now
revenue from selling, while the additional cost comes from production.

\subsection{Individual Supply}
\begin{definition}[Individual Supply Curve]\label[definition]{def:individual-supply}
An \term{individual supply curve} shows the quantity a seller is willing
and able to sell at each price, holding other conditions fixed. Quantity
is measured over a specified period; price is on the vertical axis.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Shell's Gasoline Supply]\label[example]{ex:shell}
The first supply graph in the slides gives:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrr}
\toprule
Price (\$/litre)&1.60&1.80&2.00&2.20&2.40\\
Quantity (millions of litres/day)&8&9&10&11&12\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Below \dollar{1.60}, the slides state that Shell supplies zero.
\end{example}
These data describe the example, but do not explain its jump from zero to
eight million litres. They also do not establish quantities above
\dollar{2.40}.

\paragraph{The law of supply.}
With other conditions fixed, a higher price weakly increases quantity
supplied and a lower price weakly decreases it. ``Upward sloping'' allows
unchanged quantities over some price ranges.

\subsection{Perfect Competition and Price Taking}
\begin{definition}[Perfect Competition]\label[definition]{def:competition}
The slide model of \term{perfect competition} assumes:
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item many buyers and sellers, each small relative to the market, with no
barriers to entry or exit;
\item an identical product across firms;
\item perfect information;
\item zero costs for buyers to switch sellers.
\end{enumerate}
A \term{price taker} treats the market price as given when choosing output.
\end{definition}
With identical products and costless switching, charging more than other
sellers loses buyers. A firm that can sell its chosen output at the going
price has no reason to charge less for that output. The slides also appeal
to free entry and competition to explain why profitable undercutting
opportunities would not persist.

We use price taking as the model's assumption, rather than treating this
informal argument as a complete proof about every market. Perfectly
competitive markets are uncommon in practice; the simplification lets us
study output choice without also modelling a firm's influence on price.

\subsection{The Rational Rule for Sellers}
For a price taker, the marginal benefit of selling one more unit is the
market price. Compare it with the additional cost of producing that unit.

\begin{definition}[Fixed and Variable Costs]\label[definition]{def:fixed-variable}
A \term{fixed cost} does not vary with the output level under consideration;
a \term{variable cost} does. Total cost can be written as
\[
\TC(q)=F+\VC(q),
\]
where $F$ is fixed cost and $\VC(q)$ is variable cost.
\end{definition}
When the same $F$ is incurred at both quantities, it cancels in their
comparison. Thus the marginal cost of the $k$-th unit is
\[
\MC(k)=\VC(k)-\VC(k-1).
\]
The relevant cost includes opportunity costs, not only explicit payments.
\term{Profit}, measured as revenue minus these economic costs, is the
producer's economic surplus.

\paragraph{The seller's rule.}
Sell another unit when its price is at least its marginal cost. If marginal
cost is nondecreasing, units cease to be worthwhile once their marginal
cost exceeds price; later units will not reverse that comparison.
This explains the law of supply in this model: raising price makes more
units worthwhile, while lowering it makes fewer worthwhile.

For discrete units, the last produced unit can have marginal cost below
price while the next has marginal cost above it. A unit with marginal
cost equal to price adds zero profit. The slide's equality rule describes
a smooth interior choice, rather than an equality every discrete answer
must satisfy.

\begin{remark}[Fixed Does Not Mean Sunk]
Fixed and variable describe dependence on output. Sunk and avoidable
describe whether a decision can change the payment. A fee paid only if the firm
operates can affect whether it should produce at all, even if it does not
vary with positive output. First find the best output if operating, then
compare its profit with not operating. Ignore a fixed payment only when
it is common to the options actually being compared.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Diminishing Marginal Product}
\begin{definition}[Marginal Product]\label[definition]{def:marginal-product}
A \term{production function} relates inputs to output. The \term{marginal
product} of labour is the additional output from an additional unit of
labour, with other inputs fixed. \term{Diminishing marginal product} means
successive extra units of labour add smaller amounts of output.
\end{definition}
For example, adding workers while keeping kitchen equipment fixed can
make each additional worker less productive. Total output may still rise;
it is the increment that shrinks.

If each extra worker costs the same wage but adds less output, the labour
cost per additional output unit rises. The slides identify this as one
reason marginal cost tends to increase. Rising input prices provide another
reason. The argument keeps other conditions fixed; diminishing labour
product by itself need not describe a firm's costs if it can change all
inputs or improve technology.

\subsection{The Production Example}
\begin{example}[Labour with Fixed Capital]\label[example]{ex:production}
The slides use $Y=\sqrt{LK}$, where $L$ is labour, $K$ is capital and $Y$
is output. With capital fixed at $K=4$, this becomes $Y=2\sqrt L$.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrrrr}
\toprule
Labour $L$&0&1&2&3&4&5&6\\
Output $Y$&0&2&2.83&3.46&4.00&4.47&4.90\\
Marginal product&---&2&0.83&0.64&0.54&0.47&0.43\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}
The third labour unit adds $2\sqrt3-2\sqrt2\approx0.64$ output units.
Subtracting rounded entries gives $3.46-2.83=0.63$, which differs because
of rounding. Calculate the change from exact values before rounding.
The table shows total output increasing while each additional labour unit
adds less.

To calculate the cost of a specified output, solve the same relation
for labour: $L=Y^2/4$. At a wage of \dollar{15} per labour unit,
the labour cost is $15L=3.75Y^2$. The slides' second table is:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrrrr}
\toprule
Output $Y$&0&1&2&3&4&5&6\\
Labour $L$&0&0.25&1&2.25&4&6.25&9\\
Cost (\$)&0&3.75&15&33.75&60&93.75&135\\
Marginal cost (\$)&---&3.75&11.25&18.75&26.25&33.75&41.25\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Capital remains 4, and its cost is omitted from this table. Adding a
common fixed capital cost would raise the cost totals but leave the
marginal-cost row unchanged.

The third output unit requires $2.25-1=1.25$ more labour units, costing
$15\cdot1.25=\dollar{18.75}$. It costs more than the second output unit,
which needs only $1-0.25=0.75$ more labour units and costs \dollar{11.25}.
This is the link between diminishing marginal product and rising marginal cost.

The two tables change different quantities: the first adds one labour
unit at a time, while the second adds one output unit at a time and permits
fractional labour. Their marginal rows therefore measure different things.
For a seller at price \dollar{15} per output unit, the second unit is
worth producing because its marginal cost is \dollar{11.25}; the third
is not because it costs \dollar{18.75}. Within this table, two units
maximise profit even though no marginal cost equals price.

\subsection{Market Supply}
\begin{definition}[Market Supply]\label[definition]{def:market-supply}
\term{Market supply} adds all sellers' quantities at the same price.
For $M$ sellers, $Q^S(p)=\sum_{j=1}^M q_j^S(p)$. For identical sellers,
$Q^S(p)=Mq^S(p)$.
\end{definition}
This is a horizontal sum. If each seller supplies weakly more at a higher
price, so does the sum. A higher price can also make previously inactive
potential sellers supply a positive quantity.

\begin{example}[Ten Identical Sellers]\label[example]{ex:market-supply}
The aggregation slide gives:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrr}
\toprule
Price (\$/litre)&1.60&1.80&2.00&2.20&2.40\\
Individual quantity&6&8&10&12&14\\
Market quantity&60&80&100&120&140\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Quantities are millions of litres per day. For example, at \dollar{2.20},
market quantity is $10\cdot12=120$ million litres.
\end{example}
This slide uses different individual quantities from Shell's first graph
in \cref{ex:shell}. Treat it as a separate numerical illustration.

\subsection{Movements Along Versus Shifts of Supply}
\begin{definition}[Movement and Shift]\label[definition]{def:supply-shift}
Changing a good's own price with other conditions fixed gives a
\term{movement along the supply curve}, changing \term{quantity supplied}.
Changing another relevant condition can \term{shift the supply curve},
changing \term{supply}: a different quantity is supplied at the same price.
An increase in supply shifts right; a decrease shifts left.
\end{definition}
On the original market curve, raising gasoline price from \dollar{1.80}
to \dollar{2.20} raises quantity from 80 to 120 million litres per day.
In the crude-oil example, gasoline price stays at \dollar{2.00}, but a
higher input cost lowers supply from 100 to 60 million litres per day.
The first is a movement; the second is a left shift.

\subsection{The Five Supply Shifters}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Input prices.} Higher input prices raise marginal cost and
reduce the units worth supplying at a given output price. Crude oil is an
input to gasoline, so its price rise shifts gasoline supply left.
\item \textbf{Productivity and technology.} Improvements that lower the cost
of additional output shift supply right. Examples include new equipment,
better management or production methods, and learning by doing.
\item \textbf{Prices of related outputs.} \term{Substitutes in production}
are alternative outputs made with the same inputs; a higher price for one
encourages reallocating inputs to it, reducing supply of the other.
\term{Complements in production} are produced together; a higher price
for one can encourage joint production and increase supply of the other.
These are production relationships, distinct from consumer substitutes
and complements.
\item \textbf{Expectations.} If output can be stored, an expected future
price increase can encourage withholding it today. This is more relevant
for nonperishable gasoline than fresh fish; storage costs also matter.
\item \textbf{Type and number of sellers.} Adding sellers increases the
market sum, holding existing individual curves fixed. A merger or
Competition Bureau ruling can change the seller population and other
market conditions, potentially affecting supply.
\end{enumerate}
The distinction between movement and shift depends on which price changes.
Gasoline's own price is on its supply graph; the price of crude oil is a
condition behind that graph.

\subsection{Buyers and Sellers Compared}
Both use the marginal principle. Buyers compare the value of another unit
with its price; sellers compare its price with its additional production cost.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
&Buyer&Price-taking seller\\
\midrule
Marginal benefit&Value of extra unit&Price received\\
Marginal cost&Price paid&Cost of extra production\\
Take another unit if&$\MB\geq p$&$p\geq\MC$\\
Usual quantity response&Falls as price rises&Rises as price rises\\
Underlying explanation&Diminishing marginal benefit&Rising marginal cost\\
Market curve&Sum of buyers' quantities&Sum of sellers' quantities\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
These marginal stopping rules rely on the stated pattern of benefits or
costs. The equality formulations are useful for smooth interior choices,
but should not replace the comparisons in the slides' discrete tables.

\subsubsection*{In practice}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Specify the decision.} Choosing output with costs already
committed differs from deciding whether to operate. Cancel only common costs.
\item \textbf{Keep the increment straight.} An extra worker and an extra
unit of output are different changes; identify which the table measures.
\item \textbf{Identify the price.} Own-price changes give movements along
a curve; input prices and other conditions can shift it.
\end{itemize}
