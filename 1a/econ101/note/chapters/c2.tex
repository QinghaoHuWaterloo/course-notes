\section{Demand and Consumer Choice}
Demand describes what buyers would choose at different possible prices.
It connects willingness to pay with quantity decisions, then combines
individual choices into a market relationship.

\subsection{Individual Demand}
\begin{definition}[Individual Demand Curve]\label[definition]{def:individual-demand}
An \term{individual demand curve} shows the quantity a buyer is willing
and able to buy at each price, holding other relevant conditions fixed
(\term{ceteris paribus}). Quantity is measured over a specified period.
\end{definition}
We can write this quantity as $q^D(p)$. Price is the input, but economics
graphs put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.
A demand curve describes a range of possible choices; a quantity demanded
is one choice at one price.

\begin{example}[Darren's Gasoline Demand]\label[example]{ex:darren}
The slides survey Darren about his daily gasoline use:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrr}
\toprule
Price (\$/litre)&2.40&2.20&2.00&1.80&1.60\\
Quantity (litres/day)&1&2&3&5&7\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}
These are hypothetical-price responses; connecting them does not
establish an exact formula at unobserved prices.

\paragraph{The law of demand.}
Holding other conditions fixed, a lower price weakly increases quantity
demanded, and a higher price weakly decreases it. ``Downward sloping''
allows a buyer's quantity to stay unchanged over some price ranges.
This is the course's usual demand relationship, not a statement that price
is the only influence on buying.

\subsection{The Rational Rule for Buyers}
A \term{price taker} treats the price as given rather than choosing it.
For such a buyer, an additional unit's marginal cost is its price.
The rule is to buy another unit when its marginal benefit is at least that
price, provided successive marginal benefits diminish.

\begin{definition}[Diminishing Marginal Benefit]\label[definition]{def:diminishing-benefit}
\term{Diminishing marginal benefit} means that successive additional units
provide no greater benefit than preceding units, with other conditions fixed.
It does not mean that total benefit decreases.
\end{definition}
If the extra benefit stays positive but gets smaller, total benefit is
still rising, just in smaller increments. The assumption makes it sensible
to stop once a unit costs more than it is worth: later units will not
restore a positive marginal surplus.

\begin{example}[Darren's Ranked Uses]\label[example]{ex:darren-mb}
The slides assign these benefits to successive daily litres:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{clc}
\toprule
Unit&Use&Marginal benefit (\$)\\
\midrule
1&Drive to work instead of taking the bus&2.40\\
2&Drive to school instead of biking&2.20\\
3&Shop at No Frills instead of a dearer shop&2.00\\
4&Visit friends&1.90\\
5&Drive to the gym instead of jogging there&1.80\\
6&Drive for errands instead of walking&1.70\\
7&Take a scenic drive&1.60\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}
At \dollar{1.85} per litre, the first four uses are worth at least their
cost. The fifth is worth only \dollar{1.80}, so it would reduce surplus
by \dollar{0.05}; subsequent uses are worth still less. Darren therefore
buys four litres in this example.

This explains the law of demand in the slide model: lowering the price
makes more of the ranked uses worthwhile, while raising it rules some out.
At \dollar{1.80}, the fifth litre adds exactly zero surplus. Including it
reproduces the survey's five litres, but buying four would give the same
surplus. We include units at equality when describing the tabulated choices.

The slides say to buy until marginal benefit equals price. With discrete
units, the precise stopping rule is that the last purchased unit is worth
at least the price and the next is worth less. Exact equality need not
occur. For smoothly divisible quantities, equality describes an interior
optimum when marginal benefit falls through the price; a budget or quantity
limit can instead make an endpoint optimal.

\subsection{Market Demand}
\begin{definition}[Market Demand]\label[definition]{def:market-demand}
\term{Market demand} adds all individual quantities demanded at the same
price. For $N$ buyers,
\[
Q^D(p)=\sum_{i=1}^N q_i^D(p).
\]
This is called a \term{horizontal sum}, because quantity is on the
horizontal axis.
\end{definition}
If every buyer weakly reduces their quantity when price rises, adding
those quantities gives the same direction for the market total. A price
change can affect both existing customers' quantities and whether some
potential customers buy at all. A customer moving from zero to positive
purchases is included in this comparison.

\begin{example}[Surveying 400 Consumers]\label[example]{ex:market-demand}
The slides scale a 400-person sample to a population of 40 million:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
Price&Darren&Brooklyn&Sample total&Projected millions\\
\midrule
1.60&7&4&1200&120\\
1.80&5&3&1100&110\\
2.00&3&2&1000&100\\
2.20&2&1&900&90\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Prices are dollars per litre; quantities are litres per day, with the

\end{example}
The scale factor is $40{,}000{,}000/400=100{,}000$. For example,
$1000\cdot100{,}000=100$ million litres at \dollar{2.00}.
The projection assumes that the sample represents the population; it is
an estimate, not an exact population count.

\subsection{Movements Along Versus Shifts of Demand}
\begin{definition}[Movement and Shift]\label[definition]{def:demand-shift}
A change in the good's own price, holding other conditions fixed, is a
\term{movement along the demand curve}; it changes \term{quantity demanded}.
A change in another relevant condition can \term{shift the demand curve}:
there is a different quantity demanded at the same price. This changes
\term{demand}, meaning the relationship as a whole.
\end{definition}
An increase in demand is a right shift: more is demanded at each price
in the range being compared. A decrease is a left shift. The direction
is read horizontally, even when the curve is downward sloping.

\begin{example}[Two Comparisons]\label[example]{ex:demand-comparison}
On the original market curve, lowering price from \dollar{2.20} to
\dollar{1.80} raises quantity from 90 to 110 million litres per day.
The slide's increased-demand curve instead has 120 million litres at the
same price of \dollar{1.80}, rather than 110 million.
\end{example}
The first changes price on one curve; the second compares curves at a
fixed price. Thus ``price fell, so demand increased'' should be
``price fell, so quantity demanded increased'', with other conditions fixed.

\subsection{The Six Demand Shifters}
\begin{definition}[Normal and Inferior Goods]\label[definition]{def:normal-inferior}
A \term{normal good} has higher demand when income increases; an
\term{inferior good} has lower demand, holding price and other conditions
fixed over the income range being considered.
\end{definition}
``Inferior'' describes the income response, not the good's quality.
Higher income does not automatically increase demand for every good.

\begin{definition}[Complements and Substitutes]\label[definition]{def:related-demand}
Goods are \term{complements} when lowering the price of one raises demand
for the other. They are \term{substitutes} when lowering the price of one
lowers demand for the other, holding other relevant conditions fixed.
\end{definition}
The six shifters in the slides are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Income.} The direction depends on whether the good is normal
or inferior. A tax cut raising disposable income shifts gasoline demand
right if gasoline is normal in the relevant range.
\item \textbf{Preferences.} For example, a marketing campaign can make a
brand more desirable and raise willingness to pay.
\item \textbf{Prices of related goods.} A cheaper complement raises demand;
a cheaper substitute lowers it. The price changing is the \emph{other}
good's price, so this shifts the original good's curve.
\item \textbf{Expectations.} Beliefs about future prices can change purchases
today, such as buying earlier in anticipation of a price increase.
\item \textbf{Congestion and network effects.} A \term{network effect} makes a
good more valuable as more other people use it; a \term{congestion effect}
makes it less valuable. These can change a buyer's willingness to pay.
\item \textbf{Type and number of buyers.} Adding buyers increases the market
sum, holding existing buyers' curves fixed. This direct aggregation effect
changes market demand without changing those individual curves.
\end{enumerate}
Congestion or network effects may additionally change individual curves;
the last factor isolates the direct effect of adding buyers.

\subsubsection*{In practice}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Buy another unit?} Compare its marginal benefit with price;
check how a tie is treated.
\item \textbf{Aggregate at one price.} Add quantities, with matching units
and periods, rather than adding prices.
\item \textbf{Movement or shift?} Ask whose price changed. The good's own
price gives a movement; other conditions can shift its curve.
\end{itemize}
